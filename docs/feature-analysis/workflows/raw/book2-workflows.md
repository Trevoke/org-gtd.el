# Book 2: "Making It All Work" (David Allen, 2008) — Recommended User Workflows

Source: `docs/source-material/making-it-all-work-CONVERTED.txt` (line citations refer to that file).

This book reframes GTD around two axes: **Control** (capture → clarify → organize → reflect →
engage; chapters 5–9) and **Perspective** (six Horizons of Focus; chapters 11–17). Where this
book differs from GTD (2001), it supersedes it; each entry's `refines` field records that.

**Terminology refinements over GTD 2001 (global):**
- "Collect" → **Capture** (also "collect, clear, corral"; lines 2895–2898)
- "Process" → **Clarify** ("In the original GTD workflow model, I referred to this stage as
  'processing,' but in its broadest application 'clarify' is a more encompassing word"; 3894–3896)
- "Review" → **Reflect** ("Hence I'm using 'reflect' in place of the original GTD wording of
  'review'"; 5892–5893)
- "Do" → **Engage** (chapter 9 title; 6140)
- The six Horizons of Focus are promoted from an aside (GTD 2001 ch. 2) to a co-equal half of the
  whole method, each with its own question, format, and review cadence.
- Priorities get a full treatment for the first time (Allen explicitly says GTD 2001 underplayed
  them; 6881–6896).

**NOT mined as workflows:** chapters 10 and 18 (Gracie's Gardens) are worked case-study EXAMPLES
only; they were used solely to confirm the general flows (e.g., horizon content samples at
10691–10774 use Gracie's Gardens illustratively).

## A. Fundamentals / Master Mindset

### B2-W-01 — Appropriate Engagement: the master Control + Perspective loop
- **Trigger/situation:** Any time you feel "off" instead of "on" — out of control, lacking
  direction, or both (1936–1946); applies from cooking dinner to running a company (2017–2025).
- **Prescribed steps (in order):**
  1. Recognize that there are only two things to do: "get organized and get focused… in either
     order is fine" (2017–2022) — re-conceived as **get control** and **clarify objectives at the
     appropriate horizon** (2035–2040).
  2. Apply the five stages of control (capture, clarify, organize, reflect, engage) to whatever is
     out of control (2082–2091).
  3. Apply the six Horizons of Focus to whatever lacks direction: "your actions will need to
     support projects within areas of focus and responsibility, toward whatever goals you have
     defined that will move you toward your vision, all guided by your purposes and values"
     (2086–2091).
  4. Treat the whole as holistic: "your productivity will only be as good as the weakest link in
     that chain" (2350–2358).
  5. Don't aim for permanent perfection — "The trick is not how to prevent this happening, but how
     to shorten the time you stay in an unsettled state" (2162–2164). "Do your best to capture,
     clarify, and organize what you can, have the basic conversations you need to have… and then
     just get moving. If and when you find yourself off base, course-correct and then get going
     again—ad infinitum" (6900–6906).
- **End state:** "Captain and Commander" mode — flow, no overwhelm, no work/life distinction
  (2631–2654); positive engagement where "what you're doing at any point in time is what you think
  you should be doing" (2235–2241).
- **Cadence:** Continuous; invoked whenever off-balance.
- **Source:** ch. 3 (1916–2305), ch. 4 (2308–2358), ch. 9 close (6898–6931).
- **Refines:** Elevates GTD 2001's implicit "control & perspective" into the explicit master
  framework; "work" redefined as "anything you want to get done that's not done yet" (2223–2227);
  rejects "life/work balance" as a goal (2273–2304).

### B2-W-02 — Matrix of Self-Management self-diagnosis
- **Trigger/situation:** Wanting to assess your own standing (or another person's, or a
  situation's) on the control/perspective axes, "and as a guide for improvement" (2360–2365).
- **Prescribed steps (in order):**
  1. Place yourself on the 2×2 matrix of low/high control × low/high perspective (2362–2370).
  2. Identify the quadrant syndrome:
     - **Low control / low perspective = Victim** (positive aspect: **Responder**): "dealing with
       only the latest and loudest… emergency scanning of voice mails and e-mails" (2390–2412).
     - **High control / low perspective = Micromanager** (positive: **Implementer**): "inordinate
       emphasis on structure, process, and system… Form… will overtake function" (2483–2503).
     - **Low control / high perspective = Crazy Maker** (positive: **Visionary**): "too many ideas
       in proportion to the amount getting done… overcommitment: your psychic bank account is
       overdrawn" (2579–2596).
     - **High control / high perspective = Captain and Commander**: the optimal state of flow
       (2631–2654).
  3. Accept that the matrix is "relative, situational, and fluid" — you can occupy different
     quadrants per area and per horizon, and cycle through all four in a day (2706–2742).
  4. "The first step in improving what's going on is acceptance of what is going on" (2745–2747);
     then choose strategy: need more control → five stages; need more perspective → horizons;
     possibly both (2749–2756).
- **End state:** Diagnosis of which half of the model to apply, and where.
- **Cadence:** Whenever feeling off; the labels are "warnings for a course correction" (2383–2386).
- **Source:** ch. 4, 2360–2759.
- **Refines:** NEW in this book — the Matrix of Self-Management does not appear in GTD 2001.

### B2-W-03 — "What's true right now?": start with what has your attention
- **Trigger/situation:** Not knowing where to start; attempting to "set priorities" while loose
  ends are pulling at you (2831–2835); facilitating a stuck person/team (2843–2849).
- **Prescribed steps (in order):**
  1. Ask, in some form appropriate to the situation, **"Okay, so what's true right now?"**
     (2843–2849).
  2. Begin with where you are, not with idealized priorities/strategy/values: "we suggest that you
     begin with where you are. Very few people… start off with 'Fulfill my destiny on the planet.'
     Most begin with something like 'Fix printer'" (2851–2858).
  3. Capture whatever surfaces, large or small — "the process of dealing with these blips… is
     identical for the small and the large ones" (2816–2818).
  4. Principle: "what usually most needs your attention is what most has your attention"
     (2763–2766); "If you don't pay attention to what has your attention, it will take more of
     your attention than it deserves" (2792–2797).
- **End state:** The actual current inventory is on the table, clearing the way for
  higher-horizon work ("If you don't deal with those effectively, they will undermine your
  recognition of the bigger stuff"; 2861–2866).
- **Cadence:** Whenever stuck or starting any improvement process.
- **Source:** ch. 4, 2761–2869.
- **Refines:** Makes explicit and central GTD 2001's bottom-up rationale; "pay attention to what
  has your attention" is this book's recurring master heuristic (also 7444–7453, 8455–8457).

## B. Control Stage 1: Capturing (ch. 5)

### B2-W-04 — The Personal Mind Sweep (core capturing flow)
- **Trigger/situation:** "The first thing to do, when you are feeling in any way out of sorts, is
  to clear the air by grabbing hold of whatever is pulling on your focus" (2940–2943); also the
  standard first exercise of GTD coaching (2966–2976).
- **Prescribed steps (in order):**
  1. Rule: "if it's on your mind, write it down or record it somehow in a concrete way… It doesn't
     matter how you capture these thoughts, as long as you get them out of your head and have them
     all in some way easily accessible for review" (2945–2953).
  2. **Survey the physical environment**: what's on/in/under/around the desk, shelves, cabinets,
     drawers, files, closets, briefcase, walls, boards. "Anything that isn't supplies, decoration,
     reference material, or equipment is an item they need to address" (2978–2984).
  3. For each item ask: **"Do you have any attention on this?"** — let the owner make the call; do
     not editorialize (2989–2994).
  4. Gather all incomplete material into the in-basket (or expanded zone around it) (2996–2997).
  5. Then ask: is there **anything else** on your mind not yet in the stack? Write each item **on a
     separate sheet of paper** and toss it in — "people are much more capable of focusing clearly
     on a single item and making a decision about it than attempting to work off a long running
     list" (2998–3006).
  6. Use an **Incompletion Trigger list** (Appendix i) to jog deeper layers of the psyche
     (3008–3015).
  7. Defer all analysis/decisions: "In the first phase of capturing, a best practice is to avoid
     doing analysis and decision-making, so you don't constrain the gathering process" (4038–4041).
- **Success rules for capturing** (3740–3798): *No Bad Ideas*; *Overcapture* ("There's more than
  you think in what you think"); *Stream of Consciousness*; *No Commitment* ("idea generation is
  not the same as making decisions about goals and plans"). Capturing must become "a lifestyle
  factor" (3802–3813). Tools must be instantly at hand — pen and paper beat everything most of the
  time (3632–3704). "Your mind is for having ideas, not for holding them… You should be thinking
  about your stuff, not of it" (3815–3820).
- **Variants of the same stage:** journaling (3269–3303), brainstorming = "mind-sweeping on a
  particular theme" (3305–3344), cleaning up / "When in doubt, clean a drawer" (3346–3386), and
  group capture — start regular meetings by having everyone download what has their attention
  (3388–3426).
- **End state:** Everything that has your attention is in one delimited external location (in-tray
  or list), one placeholder per item, nothing roaming "loose in the morass of the subliminal mind"
  (2933–2938). Takes "part of an hour to several hours" the first time (3017–3019).
- **Cadence:** Habitual/continuous; full sweep whenever internal pressure rises (the sitcom-writer
  story: "every time he starts to feel that his world is bigger than he is… he just sits down and
  makes another list"; 3518–3531).
- **Source:** ch. 5, 2873–3861.
- **Refines:** GTD 2001's "collect," renamed **capture** and broadened beyond in-baskets to any
  acknowledgment of "what's true, now" (2895–2902), including team/group capture.

### B2-W-05 — Higher-horizon capture sweep (Horizons of Focus as trigger checklist)
- **Trigger/situation:** After the obvious mind sweep, "more qualitatively weighty things on your
  mind… lay hidden behind the more visible ones" (3030–3037).
- **Prescribed steps (in order):** Use the Horizons of Focus as a capture checklist (3039–3046),
  scanning each level for what has your attention:
  1. **10,000 ft / Projects**: capture unrecognized projects — especially "problems" you have yet
     to recognize as projects (next holiday, elder care, kids' summer programs, son's math grades)
     (3048–3082).
  2. **20,000 ft / Areas of focus & responsibility**: review your job description; "people find at
     least two or three significant issues that have been tugging at them"; scan life areas —
     finances, health, recreation, relationships, etc. (3084–3120).
  3. **30,000 ft / Goals & objectives**: deadlines in the next months–2 years; aspirations not yet
     articulated — "the question of setting them already has a piece of your psyche attached"
     (3122–3156).
  4. **40,000 ft / Vision**: career sea-changes, possible downsizing, lifestyle dissonance
     (3158–3175).
  5. **50,000 ft / Purpose & principles**: unexpressed talents, values violations (e.g., a boss
     "behaving in ways that are outside your core values") (3177–3205).
  6. Key permission: "it's not necessary to know anything in its fullness in order to get control
     of it… When in doubt, write it down. Put it in your in-basket" (3229–3251). Fuzzy
     higher-horizon items often become "looking into" projects (3236–3242).
- **End state:** Subtle, high-altitude open loops are externalized into the same funnel as "buy
  cat food" (3199–3205).
- **Cadence:** As part of any thorough mind sweep; whenever subtle unease persists.
- **Source:** ch. 5, 3030–3262.
- **Refines:** NEW emphasis — GTD 2001's collect was mostly runway/project level; here the horizons
  themselves are a capture instrument.

### B2-W-06 — Capture as interrupt-shield and bookmarking
- **Trigger/situation:** Interruptions ("corporate drive-by", 3547–3560); juggling many open
  threads (calls, meetings) without time to fully process them (3573–3581).
- **Prescribed steps (in order):**
  1. When interrupted with a request, **write it down, throw it into your in-basket**, and trust
     you'll get to it within a few hours — instead of immediately dropping everything (3549–3560).
  2. Exception: do it now if it's genuinely the most important thing or takes under two minutes
     (3562–3565; cross-ref B2-W-14).
  3. While on calls/in meetings, take notes ("bookmarking"); on completion either toss them (no
     action) or throw them into the in-tray for later processing into actions/reference
     (3583–3607).
  4. Print agendas before meetings, annotate during, toss annotated copy into in-basket after
     (3602–3607).
- **End state:** Focus preserved; nothing lost; all threads re-enter the standard capture→clarify
  funnel.
- **Cadence:** Continuous, event-driven.
- **Source:** ch. 5, 3533–3607.
- **Refines:** Practical extension of GTD 2001 collection habits to interruptions/context-switching.

## C. Control Stage 2: Clarifying (ch. 6)

### B2-W-07 — The Clarifying decision flow (the critical questions)
- **Trigger/situation:** Processing captured "stuff" — "things that have landed in your world…
  about which you've yet to determine what you're going to do" (3957–3963); emptying in-baskets to
  zero (4108–4114). Work items one at a time from a single bucket (3917–3925, 3965–3973).
- **Prescribed steps (in order)** (summary at 4186–4191: "decide whether something is actionable
  or not, and then determine the outcomes and actions required for those things you've committed
  to move on; for those that don't have an action tied to them, decide what's trash, what's to be
  reviewed later, and what's to be classified as reference"):
  1. **What is it?** Determine its specific meaning to you ("What is it? What do you think you
     should do with it?"; 4157–4159; "What is this? Is this really worth doing? How does it fit
     with all the other things I have to do?"; 4074–4077).
  2. **Is it actionable?** — "the first distinction that must be made about anything" (4206–4210).
     "There are two possible answers here—'yes' and 'no.' 'Maybe' is actually 'no, but the item
     might require action later'" (4212–4214). Never park actionable and non-actionable items
     together — blended stacks create psychic numbness (4216–4228).
  3. **If actionable — the fundamental thought process** (4270–4283), two questions verbatim:
     - "**What's my desired outcome? What am I committed to accomplishing or finishing about
       this?**"
     - "**What's the next action? What's the next thing I need to do to move toward that goal?**"
     If more than one action is needed to close the loop, it's a project → Projects list
     (cf. App. iv, 10481–10486).
  4. **If not actionable — three sub-fates** (4478–4668):
     - **Trash** ("It's Meaningless"): "Think of it as fodder for the Delete key… wastebasket,
       recycle bin, Dumpster, shredder, or local charity" (4488–4497).
     - **Incubate** ("It's to Hold On, Until a Later Time"): "it's fine to decide not to decide"
       (4549–4552) → Someday/Maybe (no next action attached — that's its "defining
       characteristic"; 4564–4573) or date-triggered tickler (4708–4710; cf. B2-W-11).
     - **Reference**: "does not involve action, but has value as information" (4641–4648); the
       only requirement is making the judgment call to keep or not (4650–4655).
  5. Worked example of the full branch (conference-invitation letter): trash / file as reference /
     tickler 10 days / project "Research XYZ conference participation" + delegated next action —
     versus the failure mode, the "hunhhh" stack (4694–4723).
- **End state:** In-basket zones at zero (4108–4114); every input has a determined meaning and a
  home.
- **Cadence:** Daily/regular (cf. B2-W-16: 30–90 min/day of "defining" work; 6769–6778).
- **Source:** ch. 6, 3864–4750.
- **Refines:** GTD 2001's "processing" renamed **clarifying** (3894–3896); the workflow-diagram
  questions are restated with "Maybe = no, for now" made explicit and the capture/clarify split
  framed as the Visionary-vs-Implementer polarity within yourself ("Most people make a list,
  trying to do both at the same time, and it just doesn't work"; 4046–4094).

### B2-W-08 — Outcome reframing: turning fuzzy "stuff" into named projects
- **Trigger/situation:** A captured item is a topic, problem, or vague intention ("Mom", "pay
  bills", "exercise", an undecided employee issue) rather than a manageable commitment
  (4294–4303, 4371–4396).
- **Prescribed steps (in order):**
  1. Rename the topic as a completable outcome: "'Mom' is not a subject about which you can do
     anything; 'Give mom a great sixtieth birthday party' is a concrete goal" (4294–4299).
  2. For unresolved dilemmas, commit to **resolution**, not to a chosen answer: write "Resolve
     situation with Carolyn Jones" before deciding whether to fire her — "your desired outcome is
     to have the situation reach its optimal closure, either way" (4327–4339).
  3. Recognize **research projects** the moment a decision commitment exists: "the project exists
     as soon as you have a commitment to make the decision" — "R & D…" / "Look into…" projects
     (4358–4369).
  4. Recognize **process projects**: "pay bills" → "set up my electronic bill-paying system";
     "exercise" → "Set up an exercise routine"; "more time with Jonathan" → "Implement a program of
     quality time with kids" (4371–4396).
  5. Then derive the next action ("Draft list of possible invitees to Mom's party"; 4298–4300).
- **End state:** Each actionable concern has "a concrete goal line" — "It's almost impossible to
  motivate yourself or anyone else to play a game in which you're not aware of a concrete goal
  line" (4403–4408).
- **Cadence:** During every clarifying pass; revisited at the weekly review.
- **Source:** ch. 6, 4294–4426; reinforced at 20k ft (8243–8250).
- **Refines:** Expands GTD 2001 outcome-thinking with explicit named patterns (resolve-projects,
  R&D-projects, process-projects).

## D. Control Stage 3: Organizing (ch. 7)

### B2-W-09 — The Organizing map: meaning → location
- **Trigger/situation:** Clarified items need to be parked; "any attempt to be 'organized'
  involves first determining what things mean to you, and then parking those items in a category
  where those things belong" (5029–5034). Definition: "Being organized simply means that where
  things are suits what they mean to you" (4837–4847). "Meaning precedes organization" (4944–4948).
- **Prescribed category system** ("the key distinction… is to determine whether it falls under the
  heading of outcomes, actions, things incubating, support material, reference, or trash";
  5036–5039):
  1. **Outcomes** (5052–5215), top-down: Purpose statement (5097–5112); Principles/credo
     (5116–5132); Vision lists/treasure maps (5136–5144); Goals & objectives list (5148–5161);
     Areas of Focus checklists — "usually fewer than twenty items" (5165–5177); **Projects list** —
     a single index, 30–100 multi-step sub-year outcomes, reviewed at least weekly (5181–5201);
     optional high-level "Outcomes You're Waiting On from Others" (5205–5215).
  2. **Actions** (5217–5455), three branches: Calendar (see B2-W-10); **context-sorted
     next-action lists** — Calls (5330), Computer (5336), Office (5341), Home (5347), Anywhere
     (5352), Errands (5358), Agendas per person/meeting (5364), Read/Review (5373); **Waiting
     For** — everything delegated, ordered, lent, or pending from others; "Your responsibility is
     to track who's got it, confirm when they got it, and check its status" (5381–5391). Contexts
     are customizable ("Brain-dead" list, "At sailboat"; 5393–5455) — most people have 150+ actions
     (5224–5225).
  3. **Incubating** (5457–5569) — see B2-W-11.
  4. **Support material** (5571–5619): sorted by project/theme/topic; never used as the action
     reminder — "Support material needs to remain purely adjunct data—not a reminder of action"
     (5590–5601); keep active project files more accessible than archives (5613–5619).
  5. **Reference** (5621–5706): general-reference filing best practices verbatim — "Organize files
     by one A-Z index; Typeset labels; Require less than sixty seconds to make and store a new
     file; Keep drawers less than three-quarters full; Make your system fun and easy to use; Purge
     files at least yearly" (5687–5697). No "to-file" piles (5672–5679).
  6. **Trash** (5708–5776): move it where trash goes; review/recalibrate stored info regularly
     because "the meaning of things changes simply with the passage of time" (5741–5751).
- **Design constraints:** the more mundane the level, the more sophisticated the system needed
  (4996–5020); don't overcategorize — that impulse signals distrust of one's own review habit
  (5083–5091); "As Simple as Possible, but No Simpler" — deleting a category just re-blends its
  contents (5778–5801).
- **End state:** "everything that means something to you should be in its proper place"
  (4845–4847); the "extended mind" can be trusted (4787–4792).
- **Cadence:** Continuous as outputs of clarifying are filed; structural tune-ups as needed.
- **Source:** ch. 7, 4754–5836.
- **Refines:** Same buckets as GTD 2001, but with the formal definition of "organized" as
  meaning↔location matching, the explicit Outcomes hierarchy mapped to horizons, and stronger
  warnings about category blending and overcategorization.

### B2-W-10 — Calendar discipline ("hard landscape") and the daily start
- **Trigger/situation:** Time-specific and day-specific commitments; the start of each day.
- **Prescribed steps (in order):**
  1. Reserve the calendar for exactly three things: "**appointments, day-specific things to do,
     and information I need or want to be aware of on that day**" (5276–5305). Calendar contents
     mean "everything I definitely need to know and do today" (5279–5280).
  2. "These two types of activities—appointments and other to-dos that have to be handled that
     day—**should be the first things you take note of each morning** to let you know how much
     discretionary time you have for other things, if any" (5293–5296).
  3. Never put "what you'd like to get done" on a day — "If it is used for any more or any less,
     it won't be totally trusted for its designed purpose" (5305–5314).
  4. During discretionary time, work from context next-action lists: "Work from these next-action
     lists whenever your calendar lets you know you have some discretionary time" (5318–5328).
- **End state:** "at a single glance at your calendar… a trusted sense about what you can and
  can't afford to do, at that moment" (5311–5314).
- **Cadence:** Daily (first thing), consulted throughout the day (7540–7545).
- **Source:** ch. 7, 5262–5328.
- **Refines:** Restates GTD 2001's hard-landscape rule unchanged; adds the explicit morning
  sequencing (calendar first → then discretionary choice).

### B2-W-11 — Incubation: Someday/Maybe vs. date-triggered tickler
- **Trigger/situation:** Items clarified as "no action now, but maybe/later" (5461–5465); also the
  prescribed relief valve when active lists overwhelm (4600–4623).
- **Prescribed steps (in order):**
  1. Split incubating items into two structures: "items that you need to review with some
     regularity and those that need to surface only on a specific day in the future. You need two
     different structures to handle those distinct functions" (5467–5470).
  2. **Regular-review items → Someday/Maybe list**, reviewed within the weekly review (5474–5487).
     Sub-lists are fine (books to read, places to visit, "not now but soon" / "not now but later" /
     "never now"; 5500–5521). Defining characteristic: no next action attached (4564–4573).
  3. **Date-specific items → calendar entry or tickler/suspense/bring-forward file** (or an
     assistant): "determine precisely when you want to be reminded of these projects again, and
     set up triggers accordingly" (5533–5564).
  4. **Demotion flow:** when active lists exceed real capacity, review each project consciously
     and "if not, the projects should be moved to a holding tank" (Someday/Maybe) "so the clients
     can constrain their focus to a more manageable set of possibilities" (4613–4623). Works only
     if the holding tank is itself trusted and reviewed (4625–4637).
- **End state:** Active lists contain only true current commitments; possibilities are parked but
  not lost ("permission to have a parking lot for possible projects… without having to allocate
  resources to them"; 4582–4588).
- **Cadence:** Someday/Maybe reviewed weekly (Get Creative phase; 10629–10632); ticklers fire on
  their dates.
- **Source:** ch. 6 4547–4637; ch. 7 5457–5569.
- **Refines:** Same as GTD 2001, with the regular-review vs. date-trigger split made structural
  and the overwhelm→demote move prescribed explicitly.

## E. Control Stage 4: Reflecting (ch. 8)

### B2-W-12 — The reflection cadence ladder (review intervals per horizon)
- **Trigger/situation:** A populated external system: "An Unused System Is Not a System"
  (5871–5877); "to really get things off your mind, you must put them on your mind, appropriately"
  (6077–6079).
- **Prescribed steps:**
  1. Understand reflection's **dual function**: "(a) to update its contents and (b) to provide
     trusted perspective" (5903–5907) — get current (mark off done items, recognize new projects,
     renegotiate commitments; 5921–5967) and get perspective ("you can only feel good about what
     you're not doing when you know what you're not doing"; 5995–5997).
  2. Apply the interval rule: "Usually the longer the horizon, the longer the time interval you
     can allow yourself between reviews" (5985–5989).
  3. The cadence ladder (Appendix iv, verbatim; 10557–10567):
     - "Runway - current actions (**daily**)"
     - "10,000 ft. - current projects (**weekly**)"
     - "20,000 ft. - current responsibilities (**monthly**)"
     - "30,000 ft. - 1-2 year goals (**quarterly**)"
     - "40,000 ft. - 3-5 year goals (**annually**)"
     - "50,000 ft. - career, purpose, lifestyle (**annually +**)"
  4. Review calendar and action lists "daily (or whenever you could possibly do any of them)"
     (10522–10523); weekly is "the best review cycle for projects" (6034–6037).
  5. Trust your brain, not links, for cross-referencing: consistent review makes item associations
     automatic ("this kind of question almost never comes from people who are diligently reviewing
     their system contents"; 6023–6041).
- **End state:** "Thorough assessments of complete inventories of commitments promotes clarity,
  creativity, and self-trust—without fail" (6135–6136). Self-defined sufficiency: "You need to
  remind yourself of them only as often as you need to remind yourself of them" (6097–6105).
- **Cadence:** As ladder above.
- **Source:** ch. 8, 5840–6136; Appendix iv 10520–10567; Appendix vii 10691–10774.
- **Refines:** GTD 2001's "review" renamed **reflect** (5892–5893); per-horizon intervals are
  codified here far more explicitly than in 2001.

### B2-W-13 — The Weekly Review: Get Clear → Get Current → Get Creative
- **Trigger/situation:** "once a week in a regular one- to two-hour executive session with
  yourself" (7785–7787); also whenever key projects lag or you've lost grip on short-term
  priorities (7788–7793). "Its function is to get you clear, current, and creative" (7796–7801).
- **Prescribed steps (in order)** (ch. 13 narrative 7796–7856; Appendix v checklist 10571–10637):
  1. **GET CLEAR** (10573–10588):
     a. Collect Loose Papers and Materials — gather business cards, receipts, scraps into the
        in-basket.
     b. Get "IN" to zero — "Process completely all outstanding paper materials, journal and
        meeting notes, voice mails, dictation, and e-mails."
     c. Empty Your Head — "Put in writing and process any uncaptured new projects, action items,
        waiting-fors, someday/maybes, etc."
  2. **GET CURRENT** (10592–10623):
     a. Review Action Lists — mark off completed; record further steps.
     b. Review Previous Calendar Data — past 2 weeks for "remaining action items, reference data,
        etc." (7827–7831).
     c. Review Upcoming Calendar — long- and short-term; capture triggered actions; block time as
        needed (7833–7843).
     d. Review Waiting-For List — follow-ups; check off received.
     e. Review Project (and Larger Outcome) Lists — "Evaluate status of projects, goals, and
        outcomes one by one, **ensuring at least one current action item on each**"; browse plans
        and support material.
     f. Review Any Relevant Checklists.
  3. **GET CREATIVE** (10627–10637):
     a. Review Someday/Maybe List — activate ripe ones to Projects; delete dead ones.
     b. Be Creative and Courageous — "Any new, wonderful, harebrained, creative,
        thought-provoking, risk-taking ideas to add into your system?" ("the one part… for which
        you won't need any discipline at all—it'll just happen, at least if you allow enough time
        for it"; 7845–7851).
- **End state:** Renegotiated self-trust — "You need to have thought enough so you don't have to
  think—just act" (7913–7916); like pro football, six days of prep enables gut-level in-game
  responses (7918–7930).
- **Cadence:** Weekly, "ideally at the end of a workweek (although anytime will be fine)"
  (7798–7800). Variant: the **family/partner weekly review** — debrief the past week, share
  projects, compare calendars, look ahead, plan together (7974–7980).
- **Source:** ch. 13, 7780–7980; Appendix v, 10571–10637.
- **Refines:** GTD 2001's weekly review, now formalized into the named three-phase
  Clear/Current/Creative structure.

## F. Control Stage 5: Engaging (ch. 9)

### B2-W-14 — Next-action specificity test, then Do / Delegate / Defer
- **Trigger/situation:** An actionable item has been clarified; deciding what real engagement
  means (6152–6162). Lists go "listless" when entries are vague ("deal with Dad's situation" →
  "Call Roberta re: Dad's situation"; 6216–6226).
- **Prescribed steps (in order):**
  1. Ask the fundamental engagement question: "**What's the next action?**" — "the bridge from the
     invisible to the visible" (6227–6230).
  2. Apply the three-question sufficiency test (verbatim, 6268–6280):
     - "**What has to happen first?**"
     - "**What does doing look like?**"
     - "**Where does it happen?**"
     "If you can't answer all three specifically, you still have work to do."
  3. Push to physical specificity ("set meeting with Bob" — how, exactly? e-mail/call/delegate?
     "you must complete the thinking process"; 6253–6266).
  4. Then route via the **3Ds — Do, Delegate, or Defer** (6379–6390):
     - **Do** it now "in less than two minutes" — "most things that can be handled in that time
       frame would actually take longer to organize and review again than to finish in the moment"
       (6385–6394).
     - **Delegate** "anything that someone other than you can do" — down, sideways, or up
       (6396–6401); track on Waiting For (6441–6447); two-minute hand-offs by e-mail; sensitive
       ones via Agendas (6434–6439).
     - **Defer**: park the reminder on the appropriate context list (6263–6266; App. iv
       10481–10486).
- **End state:** Only longer-than-two-minute, personally-owned actions remain in your system
  (6386–6388); team litmus test passes — "Is everyone involved in agreement about the subsequent
  actions that need to be taken, and who's going to be responsible for them?" (4457–4463;
  7628–7636).
- **Cadence:** Every clarifying pass; every project review.
- **Source:** ch. 9, 6209–6453.
- **Refines:** GTD 2001's two-minute rule and 3Ds restated; the three-question specificity test is
  this book's sharper formulation.

### B2-W-15 — Choosing the action of the moment: horizons + context/time/energy
- **Trigger/situation:** More defined actions than can be done today: "How do you stay in control
  of this work load?" (6449–6453).
- **Prescribed steps (in order):** "You must continually take into account the six horizons of
  your commitments, the three limiting factors for action, and the three options about what kinds
  of actions you choose to do" (6469–6471). Explicitly rejects "a simple priority grading method
  such as A, B, C, or 1, 2, 3, or High, Medium, and Low" (6466–6468).
  1. **Strategy (horizons):** choices should align top-down — purpose/values → vision → goals →
     areas → projects → actions (6492–6526) — usually accessed bottom-up in the moment.
  2. **Limiting factor 1 — Context:** "At any single point in time, you can only do what you can
     do" — hence context-organized reminders, "the first criterion that limits your options"
     (6558–6595).
  3. **Limiting factor 2 — Time available:** match task size to the window; block time in the
     weekly review for actions needing big chunks (6599–6633).
  4. **Limiting factor 3 — Energy:** "match the task at hand to your state of mind and body";
     keep "a batch of simple little tasks… so that when your energy is depleted, you can still be
     good for something" (6637–6675).
  5. **Priority:** finally, "trust a combination of your intelligence and your intuition"
     (6864–6870), hard-wired by reflection at all horizons (6180–6185). Appendix iv: "Make choices
     about your actions based on what you can do (context), how much time you have, how much
     energy you have, and then your priorities" (10532–10535).
  6. Default bias: motion. "taking any action will give you more of a sense of control than
     hanging back in hesitation" (6920–6926).
- **End state:** Trusted moment-to-moment choices, including trusted *not-doing* ("the more I'm
  aware of what I won't be doing if I decide to lie down… the better I will rest"; 6794–6801).
- **Cadence:** Continuous — "many hundreds if not thousands of times in a day" (6473–6477).
- **Source:** ch. 9, 6455–6896; Appendix iv 10531–10555.
- **Refines:** GTD 2001's four-criteria model (context/time/energy/priority), now explicitly
  integrated with the six horizons as the priority component, and with Allen's acknowledgment that
  2001 under-treated priorities (6881–6896).

### B2-W-16 — The threefold nature of work: predefined / ad hoc / defining
- **Trigger/situation:** Deciding what *kind* of work to do at any moment (6677–6683).
- **Prescribed steps:** Choose consciously among (verbatim, App. iv 10541–10550):
  1. "do work you have previously defined" — **predefined work** from calendar and action lists
     (6687–6703);
  2. "do ad hoc work as it appears" — maintenance and surprises; legitimate *only if* judged more
     important against a trusted, complete inventory ("You must sufficiently process and organize
     to trust your evaluation of the priority of the ad hoc"; 10549–10550; 6729–6743);
  3. "take time to define your work" — processing in-trays/e-mail/voice mail to zero, "usually
     takes from thirty to ninety minutes a day" (6769–6778).
  4. When idle, clean up backlog: "A surprise is coming toward you that you can't anticipate, and
     when it hits you should have as little residue as possible in your psyche" (6755–6765).
- **End state:** Surprise-resilience — "Probably 90 percent of my usage of my own personal action
  management system is to have a quick and easy way, when an unexpected item appears, to
  renegotiate with myself" (6759–6765). Avoids permanent "emergency scanning" mode (6803–6809).
- **Cadence:** Daily; defining work 30–90 min/day.
- **Source:** ch. 9, 6677–6809; Appendix iv 10541–10550.
- **Refines:** GTD 2001's threefold work model restated with the daily time budget for defining
  work made explicit.

## G. Perspective: the six Horizons of Focus (ch. 11–17)

### B2-W-17 — Perspective sequencing: one horizon at a time, usually bottom-up
- **Trigger/situation:** Relative control achieved; now "Where do you put your focus?" (7200–7204).
  Control alone decays without perspective ("If their meaning is not frequently refreshed… they
  can easily revert to so much 'stuff'"; 7263–7273).
- **Prescribed steps (in order):**
  1. Address the horizons "in separate, dedicated processes" — "the efficacy of focusing on one
     horizon at a time", while capturing cross-horizon thoughts that surface (7306–7328).
  2. Default direction is **bottom-up**, for three reasons: the mundane end is less daunting,
     "it's usually easier to gain control on the lower rungs, and the more subtle commitments are
     more attractive to engage with when you have a trust in your ability to control their
     implementation" (7353–7361). "I've rarely seen the process work the other way around"
     (7374–7381).
  3. Override rule: "pay close attention to what most has your attention" — start at whatever
     horizon is loudest (7424–7453); top-down is right in special cases (e.g., merging two company
     cultures: values → vision → goals; 7433–7442).
  4. Remember altitude ≠ importance — it "merely represents the breadth of view"; higher horizons
     are reference points for priorities of lower ones (7464–7478).
- **End state:** A complete, integrated "road map" — each horizon's commitments captured,
  clarified, and reviewed at its interval (7296–7304).
- **Cadence:** Per-horizon cadences (B2-W-12).
- **Source:** ch. 11, 7179–7478.
- **Refines:** GTD 2001 mentioned a 6-level model briefly; this book gives each level a full
  conversation, format, and cadence, and codifies the bottom-up-with-override sequencing.

Each horizon workflow below follows the book's own template: **question / scope / formats /
how & when to engage** (announced at 7455–7462).

### B2-W-18 — Runway conversation: Next Actions
- **Question this horizon answers:** "**What do you need to do?**" (7497–7499).
- **Scope:** "all the physical, visible actions that you can take"; most people have 100+
  outstanding (7503–7510).
- **Formats:** calendar (day/time-specific) + context lists (calls, computer, office, home,
  errands, agendas) (7512–7525).
- **How/when to engage:** all day, every day; calendar whenever a where/when question arises;
  action lists "whenever you had discretionary time and wanted to be clear that you were
  considering all your options" (7527–7545). Completeness is the lever: a complete inventory makes
  you "trust both your plan of attack and your choices about the actions you're not taking"
  (7549–7555). Hardest horizon to systematize: "the lower the horizon, the more sophisticated the
  system must be" (7593–7597).
- **Alignment litmus test:** "whether there is consensus about the very next actions that need to
  be taken, by whom, and by when" (7628–7636).
- **Cadence:** "Multiple times daily; whenever a question about what to do next" (App. vii,
  10769–10770).
- **Source:** ch. 12, 7482–7675.
- **Refines:** Same runway as GTD 2001; explicitly framed as a *perspective* horizon, not just
  mechanics.

### B2-W-19 — 10,000 ft conversation: Projects
- **Question:** "**What do I need to complete?**" (7691–7694).
- **Scope:** "outcomes that can be finished within a year that involve more than one action";
  30–100 at any time across all of life and work (7697–7703). Trigger-verb checklist for finding
  them: Finalize, Implement, Research, Publish, Distribute, Maximize, Learn, Set up, Organize,
  Create, Design, Install, Repair, Submit, Handle, Resolve (7724–7749). A project is not done
  directly — only its actions are (7713–7722). Personal projects belong on the same list as
  professional ones (7765–7778).
- **Formats:** a single Projects list/index, one per line, plus separate project plans and support
  material; dedicated review time (7751–7763).
- **How/when to engage — three distinct times** (verbatim, 7780–7793):
  - "once a week in a regular one- to two-hour executive session with yourself to tune up your
    awareness of the status of all your projects"
  - "whenever you have a sense that some of your key projects are lagging behind in keeping next
    actions current and in motion"
  - "whenever you're feeling as if you've lost your grip on priorities in the short term."
  The weekly session is the Weekly Review (B2-W-13). Ensure every project has a current next
  action in the system (7822–7825).
- **Cadence:** Weekly (App. vii: "Weekly review; whenever next-action contents are not current";
  10756). "the horizon least visited in a consistent and systematic way by most people"
  (8006–8011).
- **Source:** ch. 13, 7679–8020.
- **Refines:** Same project definition as GTD 2001; the within-a-year bound is now explicitly
  justified by the weekly-review cadence ("any commitment… that can be completed in that time
  period… should probably be reviewed at least once a week"; 7706–7711).

### B2-W-20 — 20,000 ft conversation: Areas of Focus and Responsibility
- **Question:** "**What do I need to maintain?**" (8034–8037).
- **Scope:** "ten to fifteen categories in areas that you are particularly responsible for,
  interested in, or pay special attention to" (8038–8043). Job ≈ 4–7 areas (imagine the year-end
  review your boss/board will hold you to; 8052–8059); life ≈ 7–10 areas (relationships,
  household, parenting, finances, self-expression, career, service, health; 8077–8082). Contents
  "are not meant to be finished" — they generate projects and actions (8132–8136).
- **Derivation move:** examine each project/action and ask "**Why am I doing that? What area of
  interest or responsibility does it reflect?**" (8084–8089).
- **Generation move:** for any area you wish you engaged more, ask "**what project, if implemented
  or completed, would automatically get you doing more of what you want to be doing?**"
  (8243–8250) — surfacing projects like "Set up a regular exercise routine" (8222–8233).
- **Formats:** high-level job description, personal lifestyle checklist, organization chart,
  departmental structure chart, project component checklists (8102–8117).
- **How/when to engage:** "revisit a high-level checklist every month or so" (8137–8141); also on
  significant job/role change (8144–8154) and when life areas feel out of balance (8164–8172).
- **Cadence:** Monthly check-ins; performance reviews; on job/life change (App. vii, 10744–10745).
- **Source:** ch. 14, 8024–8265.
- **Refines:** GTD 2001's 20k level, given the maintain-vs-complete distinction and the two
  derivation/generation questions as explicit moves.

### B2-W-21 — 30,000 ft conversation: Goals and Objectives
- **Question:** "**What do I want to achieve?**" (8279–8281).
- **Scope:** commitments to accomplish "over the next year or two"; the sorting rule between
  project and goal is pure review-frequency: "If you really need to check the status on an item
  weekly… I would call it a project… If you can honestly conclude that you only need to reassess
  your position on a monthly or quarterly basis, then… a thirty-thousand-feet item" (8283–8294).
  Goals, like projects, are completable outcomes (8296–8300).
- **Formats:** a simple list, outline, or plan; usually under a dozen items; organizational annual
  planning/off-sites (8302–8318).
- **How/when to engage:** "rethink the substance of annual and longer goals at least once a year…
  also common to revisit the annual goals on a monthly or quarterly basis, for course correction
  and recalibration" (8320–8327). Two situations demanding reassessment: "(a) when old goals have
  been overrun and haven't been reset, and (b) when commitment to a very ambitious long-term
  vision is having trouble getting connected to reality" (8385–8396). Caution: don't set ambitious
  goals while in survival mode (8419–8438).
- **Prescribed annual ritual (personal/couple):** ~30 minutes inventorying "everything we
  accomplished and everything noteworthy that we did that year", then ~30 minutes asking "what we
  would like to have on that list at the end of the following year", captured as the new goals
  list (8336–8345).
- **Cadence:** "Annually; quarterly reviews and recalibrations" (App. vii, 10732).
- **Source:** ch. 15, 8269–8457.
- **Refines:** GTD 2001's 30k level plus the explicit review-frequency rule for classifying
  goal-vs-project and the year-end ritual.

### B2-W-22 — 40,000 ft conversation: Vision
- **Question:** "**What would long-term success look, sound, and feel like?**" (8471–8474); more
  dynamically: "If you were wildly successful in the coming years, what do you imagine or see
  yourself doing or being?" (8481–8484). Multiyear (3–5+ yr) time frames (8474–8479).
- **Formats / elicitation techniques:** off-sites with looser, expanded brainstorming (8498–8515);
  "So what do you see yourself doing five years from now?" (8517–8521); "What is the biggest and
  best thing you can imagine for yourself?" plus a best-guess timeline (8523–8526); "what-if"/
  "as-if" exercises — collaboratively write the future press article as if success already
  happened (8551–8557); personal ideal-scene lists and scripted scenarios (8559–8569); visual
  "treasure maps"/collages (8567–8573).
- **How/when to engage:** regularly (often part of annual off-sites, or every 2–3 years;
  8599–8601) and on any major transitional event — "Kids leaving the nest; death, divorce, or a
  new partner; an inheritance; a surprising job offer; an unexpected illness… should trigger
  vision rethinking" (8616–8625); also when near-term goal alignment is stuck: "Often the only way
  to get agreement and alignment about the near future is to get clarity on the longer one"
  (8642–8649).
- **Calibration rule:** the held image "ha[s] to be at least 51 percent credible to achieve
  stickiness in the psyche" — ambitious but believable (8674–8680).
- **Linkage move:** "reverse-engineering it back to short-term goals and objectives, for which you
  can then create projects that themselves trigger next actions… What's the outcome? What's the
  next action?" (8682–8689). Alternatively, simply hold the vision until it reaches critical mass
  (8691–8705).
- **Cadence:** Annually (App. iv, 10565); event-driven.
- **Source:** ch. 16, 8461–8710.
- **Refines:** GTD 2001's 40k level expanded with concrete elicitation formats, the 51%-credibility
  rule, and the reverse-engineering cascade.

### B2-W-23 — 50,000 ft conversation: Purpose and Principles
- **Questions:** "**Why am I (are we)?** and **How am I (are we)?**" — purpose "provides the
  ultimate intentionality for existence and direction; principles represent the core values to be
  maintained en route" (8730–8738).
- **Purpose flow:**
  - Scope: ask "Why?" of any endeavor — "What is the reason for this activity or pursuit? Why are
    we doing it? What are we hoping or trying to accomplish?" (8744–8747); purpose exists both as a
    completable ultimate goal and as continuously-manifested reason-for-being (8749–8768).
  - Formats: mission statement / statement of purpose from leadership off-sites; personally,
    "I exist as a human being to…" (8780–8803).
  - When to engage: "You never need to know why you're doing something unless and until you do"
    (8816–8817) — triggers are (a) the very start of an endeavor (8830–8836), (b) unclear
    involvement, (c) resource-allocation conflicts ("when you have to start to make choices about
    how you spend a limited budget or… a limited portion of your time"; 8838–8845), and (d) "when
    all else associated with it fails, the best question to ask is, 'Why am I (are we) doing
    this?'" (8855–8858). Don't force it prematurely — purpose statements typically take years to
    articulate (8880–8905).
- **Principles flow:**
  - Elicitation questions verbatim: "What would have to be true about a situation for you not to
    really care where you worked or what you were doing?"; "You wouldn't care where you live as
    long as…?" (8947–8955). Organizational frame: "We are at our best when what's true?"
    (8970–8972).
  - Formats: published values lists; personal affirmations/credo; family "rules of engagement"
    (9010–9036).
  - When to engage: at the **front end** of relationships and ventures — mergers, partnerships,
    new hires, even houseguests ("What we consider really important in our day-to-day activities
    are…", "What I want you to know about how I work is…", "What would most bother us in this
    alliance would be…"; 9064–9077); and when violations surface — "you often become aware of what
    your own values are only when someone else violates them" (9106–9113).
- **End state:** "the more solid your reference point for the times when you have to make tough
  choices. Is this decision really in keeping with my purpose? Does it line up with what I
  consider really important?" (9115–9121).
- **Cadence:** "Annually +" (App. iv, 10567); App. vii: "Whenever additional clarity, direction,
  alignment, and motivation are needed" (10703–10704).
- **Source:** ch. 17, 8714–9121.
- **Refines:** GTD 2001's 50k level split cleanly into the Purpose track and the Principles track,
  each with its own trigger conditions and verbatim elicitation questions.

### B2-W-24 — Category corral: control-then-perspective on one runaway category
- **Trigger/situation:** A single category of life sprawled everywhere (the book's example:
  reading material — "Piles of books, printouts, brochures, manuals, magazines, catalogs…";
  7215–7221).
- **Prescribed steps (in order):**
  1. **Control first:** dedicate one labeled location ("a stacking tray, a box, a shelf") to the
     category, e.g., "Read/Review", and "collect every single item in your universe that fits that
     description. Don't neglect anything—gather it all" (7223–7229).
  2. **Then perspective:** interrogate the corralled contents — "What, of all the things you've
     captured that you think you ought to read, should you really be reading?… What magazine
     subscriptions should you cancel? What new magazines should you subscribe to, given your
     current aspirations and curiosities?" (7237–7245).
  3. Note the ordering rule: asking the perspective question before control is wasted effort
     (7247–7259); skipping the perspective step lets the category sprawl again (7263–7273).
- **End state:** One bounded, meaning-refreshed category; pattern is generalizable to any category
  of stuff.
- **Cadence:** As needed per category.
- **Source:** ch. 11, 7215–7292.
- **Refines:** A new, concrete micro-pattern demonstrating the book's control-before-perspective
  thesis.

## H. Project planning

### B2-W-25 — Natural Planning Model (per-project planning flow)
- **Trigger/situation:** A project needs more clarity or more motion than the bare Projects-list
  entry provides.
- **Prescribed steps (in order)** (Appendix ii, verbatim headings; 10259–10304):
  1. **Purpose/Guiding Principles** — "Why is this being done? What would 'on purpose' really
     mean?… What rules do we play by?"
  2. **Mission/Vision/Goal/Successful Outcome** — "What would it be like if it were totally
     successful? How would I know?"
  3. **Brainstorming** — "What are all the things that occur to me about this?… Be complete, open,
     nonjudgmental, and resist critical analysis." (Supported by the Project Planning Trigger List,
     Appendix iii, 10325–10456.)
  4. **Organizing** — "Identify components (subprojects), sequences, and/or priorities."
  5. **Next Actions** — "Determine next actions on current independent components… If more
     planning is required, determine the next action to get that to happen."
- **Steering rules (verbatim):** "If your project needs more clarity, **raise** the level of your
  focus. If your project needs more to be happening, **lower** the level of your focus"
  (10310–10313). Sufficiency test: "If the project is off your mind, planning is sufficient. If
  it's still on your mind, then more is needed" (10319–10321).
- **End state:** Project planned only as much as needed to be off your mind, with owned next
  actions on independent components.
- **Cadence:** At project start and whenever a project is stuck/unclear.
- **Source:** Appendix ii (10259–10321), Appendix iii (10325–10456); the model mirrors the
  Horizons cascade (purpose→vision→brainstorm/organize→action).
- **Refines:** GTD 2001's Natural Planning Model carried forward unchanged in steps; the
  raise/lower-focus steering rule and off-your-mind sufficiency test are stated more crisply here.

## Cross-reference: canonical condensed flow (Appendix iv, "Mastering Work Flow", 10460–10567)

Appendix iv restates the entire control loop in checklist form and is the best single acceptance-
test source: Collect (leakproof buckets, as few as possible, emptied regularly) → Process (what
does it mean? actionable? → do <2 min / delegate + Waiting For / defer to list; multi-step →
Projects list) → Organize (Projects, Calendar, Next Actions, Waiting For + subcategories,
longer-horizon lists, checklists, reference, on-hold/tickler, project support) → Review (calendar
and action lists daily; customized weekly review to "get clean, get current, and get creative";
longer horizons as required) → Do (context, time, energy, then priorities; predefined / ad hoc /
defining work; horizon recalibration at daily/weekly/monthly/quarterly/annual intervals).
Note: Appendix iv retains the older verbs "Collect/Process/Review/Do" even though the body
chapters rename them Capture/Clarify/Reflect/Engage — the body-text renames are authoritative for
this book.
