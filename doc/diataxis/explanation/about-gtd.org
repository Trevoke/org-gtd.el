* About GTD
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: about-gtd
:END:

org-gtd implements a methodology, not just a workflow. To use the
package well it helps to understand what the methodology is for and
which of its ideas the package models directly versus which it leaves
to you. This document is reflection, not instruction; when you want
to *do* something, the cross-links at the end will point you at the
right tutorial or how-to.

** Why GTD exists at all

David Allen's /Getting Things Done/ (2001) is, at its core, a single
claim: the human mind is excellent at *having* ideas and terrible at
*holding* them. Every commitment you keep in your head occupies a
slice of attention even when you are not actively thinking about it.
The solution is to move those commitments out of your head and into a
system you trust enough to stop checking.

The product GTD sells is not a list. It is the calm that comes from
knowing nothing important is forgotten.

#+begin_quote
"Your mind is for having ideas, not holding them." --- David Allen
#+end_quote

That framing tells you what "good org-gtd usage" looks like. If
capturing feels like a chore, or your agenda makes you anxious, the
system is failing at its real job. Method-purity is not the goal;
trust is.

** The five steps

Allen's methodology has five activities. They are sequential at the
level of a single item but cyclical at the level of a practice.

1. *Capture.* Get every open loop --- task, idea, worry, errand ---
   out of your head and into an inbox. The inbox is a single trusted
   bucket. ~org-gtd-capture~ provides one.
2. *Clarify.* Look at each captured item and decide what it actually
   means. Is it actionable? If yes, what is the very next physical
   action? If no, is it reference material, someday/maybe, or trash?
   This is the step most people skip and the step that does the most
   cognitive work.
3. *Organize.* Once you know what an item is, put it where it
   belongs: a project, a calendar slot, a delegation, a reference
   note. Org-gtd's transient organize menu is the dispatcher for this
   step.
4. *Reflect.* Periodically review the whole system --- weekly is
   Allen's prescription --- so it stays trusted. Stale lists become
   anxiety; reviewed lists become certainty.
5. *Engage.* Look at what is actually in front of you right now and
   pick something to do.

These map onto org-gtd commands directly: ~org-gtd-capture~,
~org-gtd-clarify-item~, ~org-gtd-organize~ (and the inbox-processing
loop ~org-gtd-process-inbox~), ~org-gtd-review~, and
~org-gtd-engage~. The naming is intentional --- learning the package
and learning the methodology reinforce each other.

If you have never read /Getting Things Done/, do. This document is a
sketch; the book is the canonical source.

** The categories

Clarify produces a small set of decisions, and those decisions are the
package's type system. Each one represents a different relationship
between you and the work.

- *Next action* --- a single, concrete physical step you could take
  the next time you sit down. "Email Alice for project budget," not
  "follow up with Alice." Next actions are the atoms of GTD.
- *Project* --- any outcome that requires more than one next action.
  In Allen's vocabulary "project" is much smaller than the everyday
  word: replacing a lightbulb is not a project, but "have the kitchen
  repainted" is. Projects exist to /generate/ next actions; org-gtd
  uses ~org-edna~ to chain tasks so completing one reveals the next.
- *Calendar* --- something tied to a specific moment. The defining
  test is: if it doesn't happen at that time, it doesn't happen.
  Dentist appointments belong here; "do laundry sometime Saturday"
  does not.
- *Delegated (waiting for)* --- the work has moved to someone else,
  but you still own the outcome. The waiting list is the thing that
  prevents items from disappearing into other people's inboxes.
- *Someday/Maybe* --- not committed, but worth keeping in view. The
  honest answer for a lot of inbox items. Pulling something onto the
  someday list is a real decision, not a deferral; it says "I am
  choosing not to act on this now and I want to revisit it later."
- *Reference (knowledge)* --- not a task at all. Information you
  keep. In org-gtd this is the =knowledge= type, which archives the
  heading rather than carrying it as work.
- *Trash* --- not worth keeping in any form. Naming this as a
  category matters; "should I just delete this?" is a real
  clarify-step decision.

The package adds three GTD-adjacent types: *habit* (recurring tasks
on org-mode's habit system), *tickler* (deferred items that re-enter
the inbox at a future date), and *quick-action* (the "if it takes
less than two minutes, do it now" shortcut). They aren't in the
original methodology but they fit its spirit.

For an exhaustive description of what each type stores and how, see
the [[#type-registry][type registry reference]]; for the design
reasoning behind the closed set, see
[[#about-the-type-system][About the type system]].

** Horizons of focus

Allen also describes a vertical axis he calls the /Horizons of Focus/,
running from the runway of daily action up through a 50,000-foot view
of your life's purpose:

- *H1 --- Projects* (current commitments)
- *H2 --- Areas of Focus and Accountability* (the standing roles and
  domains that generate projects: career, family, health, finances)
- *H3 --- Goals and Objectives* (1--2 year horizon)
- *H4 --- Vision* (3--5 years)
- *H5 --- Purpose and Principles* (life-long)

Projects should be in service of areas, areas in service of goals,
and so on; the higher horizons keep the lower ones honest.

org-gtd does not model the horizons. They are reflective; the package
is operational. The one exception is ~org-gtd-areas-of-focus~, which
lets you list your H2 areas and assign one to a project or task
during clarification. That single hook keeps the "which area does
this serve?" question in the workflow without the package pretending
to manage your life vision for you.

** How org-gtd lands the methodology in org-mode

GTD is method-agnostic; Allen wrote it before electronic systems were
common. The choices org-gtd makes when mapping the method onto
org-mode are deliberate:

- Categories live in an org property, =ORG_GTD=, on the heading
  (=ORG_GTD: Actions=, =ORG_GTD: Projects=, and so on).
- TODO keywords carry the action state (~NEXT~, ~TODO~, ~WAIT~,
  ~DONE~, ~CNCL~). Org-gtd does not invent its own state machine; it
  rides on org-mode's, with ~org-gtd-keyword-mapping~ telling it
  which of /your/ keywords mean which GTD state.
- Refile targets are top-level headings in =org-gtd-tasks.org=,
  identified by an =ORG_GTD_REFILE= property. Categorizing is a
  refile, reusing one of org-mode's most battle-tested primitives.
- The agenda is the engage view. ~org-gtd-engage~ is a custom agenda
  command --- everything you know about org-agenda transfers.

The further org-gtd stays from reinventing org-mode, the better it
composes with the rest of your Emacs.
